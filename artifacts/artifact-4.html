Is Your Data Ready for Machine Learning? A Checklist From Three Real-World Scenarios

AIML-500 · Data Challenges in Machine Learning · November 2026

Where common data challenges show up across the machine learning lifecycle.

Introduction

Most machine learning projects that fail do not fail because of the algorithm. They fail because of the data: duplicates that inflate test scores, historical labels that carry old biases, or data that cannot be shared safely. As an analytics engineer, checking data before anyone relies on it is my everyday job. This guide applies that same habit to machine learning.

Description

A practical data readiness guide built from three realistic scenarios: cleaning 800,000 customer reviews, scaling a résumé-screening model to a new country, and two hospitals training a model together without sharing patient records. For each one, I explain what can go wrong, what to check, and how to validate the result. The page ends with a checklist teams can use before training any model.

Objective

I wanted to show that preparing data for machine learning is a series of decisions, not a cleanup step. My goal was to give data teams a short, reusable way to spot data quality, bias, and privacy problems before they become model problems.

Process

  1. Worked through three scenarios with an AI coach. I proposed an approach for each scenario, answered follow-up questions, and revised my thinking when a probing question showed a gap.
  2. Mapped 14 common data challenges to the ML lifecycle. I grouped challenges like drift, imbalance, labeling and interoperability by the stage where they usually appear, shown in the diagram above.
  3. Connected each scenario to my own work. I added examples from data pipelines I have built, where the same problems show up outside of machine learning.
  4. Turned the lessons into a checklist. I kept only the questions a team can actually answer before training a model.

Scenario 1: Cleaning 800,000 customer reviews

What can go wrong: Duplicates, missing fields, mixed languages, emoji-only reviews, bot spam, and sentiment labels taken from star ratings. Near-duplicate reviews in both the training and test sets make the model look better than it is.

What I learned: My first instinct was to translate every review into one language. I revised that after realizing it would remove meaning and underrepresent the customers the model most needs to understand. The better rule is to normalize the format, not the meaning.

From my work: I profile data before changing anything, then compare the same counts after cleaning. Measuring reviews by language and sentiment before and after cleaning shows whether a whole group was quietly removed.

Scenario 2: Scaling a résumé-screening model to a new country

What can go wrong: The model learned from ten years of one company’s hiring decisions. Past hiring is not the same as job success, and the model can copy old bias through résumé vocabulary even when gender or region is never a column. A similar problem led Amazon to stop using an experimental recruiting tool that penalized résumés mentioning women’s activities (Dastin, 2018).

What I learned: Overall accuracy can hide failure. If 90% of applicants are rejected, a model that rejects everyone scores 90% accuracy while finding no good candidates. Results need to be broken down by error type and by group, and tested in a small pilot before scaling.

Scenario 3: Two hospitals building one model

What can go wrong: Sensitive patient records, different coding practices, and neither hospital willing to move raw data.

What I learned: Federated learning keeps records inside each hospital and shares model updates instead (McMahan et al., 2017). A common data model maps both hospitals’ codes to the same definitions. But updates can still leak information or be tampered with, so the design needs anomaly checks on updates, secure aggregation, clear governance, and testing at each hospital separately.

From my work: On a demand planning project, two teams used different fiscal calendars and their numbers never matched. Agreeing on shared definitions first, like a common data model, is what fixed it.

Common problems and quick fixes

ProblemWhat it does to a modelA common fix
DuplicatesWastes training time and inflates test scoresSimilarity-based record matching; keep related records in one split
Missing dataGaps the model cannot learn fromImputation, deletion, or a “was missing” flag
Noisy or inconsistent dataLearns wrong patterns, overfitsStandard formats, validation rules, outlier checks
Class imbalanceIgnores the minority classOversampling such as SMOTE (Chawla et al., 2002), class weights
Biased historyRepeats unfair past decisionsAudit labels, check subgroup errors, human review
Data driftAccuracy drops after launchMonitoring and scheduled retraining

A checklist before you train

  1. Do I have a saved, untouched copy of the raw data and a record of where it came from?
  2. Have I profiled the data before cleaning it, and compared it again afterward?
  3. Are the labels trustworthy, or do they carry past decisions I do not want to repeat?
  4. Could related or duplicate records end up in both the training and test sets?
  5. Will I check results by group and by error type, not just overall accuracy?
  6. Who is allowed to use this data, for what purpose, and how is access protected?
  7. How will I notice if the data changes after the model is deployed?

Documenting these answers alongside the dataset is good practice, in the spirit of datasheets for datasets (Gebru et al., 2021).

Value Proposition

This guide helps data teams catch data quality, bias, and privacy problems before training a model, so they spend less time fixing models that were never going to work and more time building ones people can trust.

Unique Value

Most guides to machine learning start at the model. This one starts at the data, from the point of view of someone who builds data pipelines every day. It shows that the habits analytics engineers already use, like profiling, validating, and agreeing on shared definitions, are exactly what makes machine learning reliable.

Relevance

Every organization adopting AI depends on the quality of its data. Data engineers, analysts, and data scientists need a shared way to decide whether data is ready. This checklist works for any team, whether they are training their first model or reviewing one that is already in production.
